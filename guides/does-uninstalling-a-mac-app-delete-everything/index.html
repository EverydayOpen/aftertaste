Does uninstalling a Mac app delete everything?

The short answer

  1. No. Dragging an app to the Trash removes the app bundle. What the app wrote in your Library stays, and Apple says so: deleting an app does not remove the documents or other files you made with it.
  2. For most apps something stays. In Homebrew's cask data, 4,734 of 7,772 casks list leftover paths, and the folders named most often are Preferences, Application Support, Saved Application State and Caches.
  3. Some of it rebuilds itself and some of it is your own data, so sort it before you remove anything, and move it to the Trash instead of deleting it.

What the Trash removes

The app itself: the .app bundle in Applications, or in the Applications folder of your home folder. If an app ships its own uninstaller, Apple's advice is to use that, because it can remove login items, extensions and other data the app stored elsewhere. If it does not, you drag the app to the Trash, and the space is freed when you empty the Trash.

Nothing else is removed. The app's preferences, caches, saved window state, containers, logs and helpers are separate files in other folders.

What stays, by folder

A cask is a Homebrew recipe for installing a Mac app, and many carry a zap entry: the author's list of what an uninstall leaves behind. We counted the public catalogue on 3 October 2026. It is a hint from people who maintain apps, not a complete inventory. The percentages below are the share of the 4,734 casks with a zap entry that list that kind of place. The tag is the highest tier Aftertaste gives an exact bundle-ID match there.

  • Preferences~/Library/Preferences/<bundle ID>.plist75.5 %High

    The app's settings. A reinstall starts with defaults. A licence key or a sign-in can live here.

  • Application Support~/Library/Application Support/<ID or name>64.9 %Medium

    Databases, licences, plug-ins, project files. The most likely place for your own data, and the most shared one.

  • Saved Application State~/Library/Saved Application State/<ID>.savedState48.1 %High

    Window positions and what was open. Rebuilt automatically.

  • Caches~/Library/Caches/<ID>46.5 %High

    Copies of things the app can fetch or compute again. Rebuilt automatically.

  • HTTPStorages~/Library/HTTPStorages/<ID>24.1 %High

    Cookies and credentials kept for the app's web requests. You would sign in again.

  • Logs~/Library/Logs/<ID or name>18.8 %High

    Old logs and crash reports. Nothing depends on them. A match by name only is Review.

  • Containers~/Library/Containers/<ID>14.7 %Medium

    A sandboxed app's whole world, including documents it kept for you. Often the only copy.

  • Application Scripts~/Library/Application Scripts/<ID>14.0 %Medium

    Scripts for sandboxed apps and extensions. You may have written them.

  • WebKit~/Library/WebKit/<ID>10.5 %High

    Local storage and caches of web views inside the app.

  • Group Containers~/Library/Group Containers/<Team ID>.<name>5.7 %Review

    Shared by an app, its extensions and other apps from the same developer. Review at best, never ticked.

  • LaunchAgents~/Library/LaunchAgents/<label>.plist2.0 %Hands off

    Starts a background job at login. Removing the file does not stop a job that is already loaded.

Outside your home folder, in /Library, the same cask data names helpers and daemons (about 1.0 % list a launch daemon, 0.4 % a privileged helper) and installer receipts (0.1 %). Those need an administrator to change, so Aftertaste lists them, with the reason, and does not remove them.

How to look yourself

  1. Open the Library

    In Finder, choose Go › Go to Folder, type ~/Library and press Return. The Library is hidden by default; holding Option while you open the Go menu also shows it.

    ⇧⌘G

  2. Find the app's bundle ID

    It is a reverse-domain name such as com.example.orbitmeet. Right-click the app, choose Show Package Contents, open Contents/Info.plist and read CFBundleIdentifier. Or, with the app still installed: mdls -name kMDItemCFBundleIdentifier /Applications/Name.app.

    CFBundleIdentifier

  3. Search each folder for that ID

    Look in the folders above for an item named with the ID. A name that only resembles the app is weaker evidence than an exact ID.

    exact id

  4. Move, don't delete

    Drag what you are sure of to the Trash. You can put it back until you empty it.

    Trash

What is worth keeping

  • Settings you may want back. If you might reinstall the app, its Preferences file carries your choices.
  • Documents inside Application Support or a Container. Notes, recordings, databases and project files sometimes live there. Open the folder and look before you move it.
  • Licence files. Some apps keep a licence or an activation record in Application Support or Preferences. Keep it if you paid for the app and may come back.
  • Anything for a subscription. Apple notes that deleting an app does not cancel a subscription bought through it.

What no app sees

Even a careful look in the Library misses records that live elsewhere: keychain items, login and background item records, Launch Services and Spotlight entries, iCloud copies, other users' accounts, and backups and snapshots. Aftertaste says "Not covered" about all of them. The Library is also not fully readable to an ordinary app: macOS protects some folders, mostly Containers and Group Containers, and a good tool says "protected by macOS" instead of "nothing found".
